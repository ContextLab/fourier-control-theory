This page is a playground for one idea: a robot arm whose joints spin at constant rates is a Fourier series, drawn by hand. The tutorial below walks through the Fourier-analysis side, the control-theory side, and then the dictionary that translates between them. Skim the "quick tour" first, then come back to any section while you play with the demo above.

0. Quick tour

The demo has three tabs.

  • Arm. An animated chain of links (the "puppet") on the left and its power/amplitude spectrum on the right. They are two views of the exact same data.
  • Draw. Sketch any closed-ish shape freehand; the app decomposes it into rotating components and re-draws it as spinning links, the way 3Blue1Brown's epicycle animations do.
  • Control. The same arm, relabeled with control-theory language: joint-angle traces, a bandwidth (low-pass) slider, and an optional feedback-lag filter.

Interactions that work in every tab that shows the arm or the spectrum:

  • Drag a joint's tip in the arm view to move it; every link further out on the chain stays rigidly attached and slides along with it.
  • Drag a stem in the spectrum view: vertical motion changes that component's amplitude, horizontal motion changes its frequency (it snaps to the nearest integer), and dragging the small ring around a selected stem's head changes its phase.
  • Double-click empty space in the spectrum to add a new component; shift-drop one stem onto another at the same frequency to merge them into one (their coefficients add).
  • In Draw mode, the K slider controls how many of the strongest components are kept in the reconstruction, so you can watch the sketch resolve as K grows.
  • In Control, the bandwidth slider truncates the series to frequencies within it, and the feedback lag toggle applies a first-order low-pass filter to every component at once.

1. Fourier analysis

1.1 Rotating phasors and the Fourier series

Treat the plane as the complex plane, so a path is a complex-valued function of time $z(t)$. A phasor at frequency $f$ is a unit-rate rotation $e^{i2\pi f t}$: as $t$ increases from $0$ to $1$, it sweeps once around the unit circle counter-clockwise if $f>0$. Scaling it by a complex coefficient $c_f = |c_f|e^{i\varphi_f}$ stretches it to radius $|c_f|$ and starts it at angle $\varphi_f$ when $t=0$.

A (complex) Fourier series is a sum of such phasors, one per integer frequency:

$$ z(t) = \sum_{f=-\infty}^{\infty} c_f\, e^{i2\pi f t}, \qquad t \in [0,1). $$

Every $c_f$ traces its own little circle at its own constant rate; the point you actually see, $z(t)$, is the running sum — the tip of the last phasor, chained onto the tip of the one before it. That chaining is exactly how the arm view is drawn.

1.2 Recovering the coefficients: orthogonality

The phasors are orthonormal over one period,

$$ \int_0^1 e^{i2\pi f t}\, e^{-i2\pi g t}\, dt = \begin{cases}1 & f = g\\ 0 & f \ne g\end{cases} \quad (f, g \in \mathbb{Z}), $$

so multiplying $z(t)$ by $e^{-i2\pi f t}$ and integrating kills every term except the one we want:

$$ \int_0^1 z(t)\, e^{-i2\pi f t}\, dt = \sum_g c_g \int_0^1 e^{i2\pi (g-f) t}\, dt = c_f. $$

That integral is the analysis formula; the sum in §1.1 is the synthesis formula. They are inverses of each other.

1.3 From an integral to the DFT

We only ever have finitely many samples, so the demo replaces the integral with a sum over $N$ uniformly-spaced samples $z_n = z(n/N)$, $n = 0,\dots,N-1$:

$$ c_f = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1} z_n\, e^{-i2\pi f n / N}, \qquad f = -\tfrac{N}{2}, \dots, \tfrac{N}{2}-1, $$

which is the discrete Fourier transform (DFT), normalized so that $c_f$ has the same units and meaning as the continuous coefficient above.

Why resample by arc length? When you draw freehand, the pointer reports points unevenly in time — slow strokes pile up samples, fast strokes skip over whole stretches of curve. The DFT above assumes evenly-spaced samples of a single lap around the curve; feeding it raw, unevenly-timed pointer samples would silently over-weight the parts of the shape you drew slowly. So before taking the DFT, the drawn curve is closed up and resampled at $N$ points equally spaced by cumulative arc length, which depends only on the shape you drew, not on how fast your hand moved.

1.4 Amplitude vs. power, and Parseval

$|c_f|$ is the amplitude spectrum: it's exactly the radius of link $f$ in the arm view. $|c_f|^2$ is the power spectrum. They rank components identically (power is a monotonic function of amplitude), but power adds up meaningfully: Parseval's theorem says

$$ \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1} |z_n|^2 \;=\; \sum_{f} |c_f|^2, $$

so the mean squared "size" of the path is literally partitioned across frequencies — the power spectrum is a budget, and truncating to the top-$K$ components (as Draw mode does) keeps as much of that budget as possible for a given number of links.

1.5 Convergence and Gibbs' phenomenon

As you keep more terms (raise $K$, or widen the bandwidth in the Control tab), the partial sum converges to $z(t)$ pointwise wherever the curve is smooth. At a corner or a discontinuity — a square wave's edge, a heart's cusp — the partial sum always overshoots by about $9\%$ of the jump, no matter how many terms you add; the overshoot gets narrower but not shorter. That's Gibbs' phenomenon, and you can watch it as a persistent little "ring" near sharp corners even at high $K$.

1.6 Negative frequencies spin the other way

$f>0$ makes $e^{i2\pi f t}$ sweep counter-clockwise as $t$ grows. Flip the sign, $f<0$, and $e^{i2\pi f t}=e^{-i2\pi |f| t}$ sweeps clockwise instead. A real-valued signal (say, just the $x$-coordinate of a drawing) needs both a $+f$ and a $-f$ component of equal amplitude to keep the imaginary parts canceling; a genuinely complex, two-sided path (like an arm's end effector) is free to use them asymmetrically, and does — that asymmetry is what makes the traced path curl one way more than the other.

2. Control theory

2.1 A planar serial manipulator

Picture a chain of $n$ rigid links connected by revolute (rotating) joints, all lying flat in the plane, base pinned at the origin. Link $k$ has a fixed length $r_k$ and, at time $t$, points in the absolute direction $\theta_k(t)$ (measured from a fixed reference axis, same for every link). Adding the link vectors tip-to-tail gives forward kinematics — the position of every joint, including the end effector — as a sum of complex link vectors:

$$ p_k(t) = \sum_{j=1}^{k} r_j\, e^{i\theta_j(t)}. $$

If each joint spins at a constant absolute rate, $\theta_k(t) = \varphi_k + 2\pi f_k t$, this is identical in form to the Fourier partial sum in §1.1, with $c_k = r_k e^{i\varphi_k}$. That's the whole analogy in one equation; §3 spells out the dictionary.

2.2 Joint space vs. task space

The $n$ joint angles together are joint space: an $n$-dimensional description of the arm's configuration. What we usually care about — where the end effector actually is, $p_n(t)$ — lives in 2-dimensional task space. Forward kinematics is the map from joint space to task space; it is easy (just evaluate the sum above) and always well defined.

2.3 Inverse kinematics and redundancy

Going the other way — given a target point $P$ in task space, find joint angles that reach it — is inverse kinematics, and it is generally hard. When $n>2$, joint space (dimension $n$) is bigger than task space (dimension $2$): infinitely many joint configurations put the tip at the same $P$. Such an arm is redundant, and the leftover $n-2$ degrees of freedom (formally, the null space of the arm's Jacobian) can move the joints around without moving the end effector at all.

Dragging in the arm view solves the smallest possible inverse-kinematics problem: moving joint $k$'s own tip to a point $P$ changes only link $k$'s amplitude and phase (two unknowns for two coordinates), leaving every other link's own parameters untouched — see §3.3 for the formula and why the rest of the chain still moves rigidly.

2.4 Absolute vs. relative (motor) angles

$\theta_k$ above is an absolute angle, measured against the fixed world frame. But a real motor at joint $k$ is bolted to link $k-1$, not to the world — it can only sense and drive the angle between link $k$ and link $k-1$. That's the relative (motor) angle,

$$ q_k(t) = \theta_k(t) - \theta_{k-1}(t), \qquad \theta_0 \equiv 0. $$

With constant absolute rates $\theta_k = \varphi_k + 2\pi f_k t$, the motor's angular velocity is $\dot q_k = 2\pi (f_k - f_{k-1})$ — the difference of neighboring frequencies, not $f_k$ itself. Two adjacent links can each be spinning fast in the world frame while the motor between them barely moves, simply because $f_k \approx f_{k-1}$.

2.5 Feedback, frequency response, and Bode plots

A feedback controller measures the error between a desired output and the actual one and corrects the actuator accordingly; how well it tracks a signal typically depends on that signal's frequency content. The standard way to describe this is the system's frequency response $H(f)$: feed in $e^{i2\pi f t}$, and (for a linear, time-invariant system) the steady-state output is $H(f)\, e^{i2\pi f t}$ — same frequency, but scaled by $|H(f)|$ and shifted in phase by $\arg H(f)$. A Bode plot shows $|H(f)|$ and $\arg H(f)$ against $f$, and is the standard way engineers read off how a controller will treat any given frequency at a glance.

2.6 Bandwidth and the first-order lag

A system's bandwidth is (roughly) the range of frequencies it can track without much attenuation or delay; components above it get squashed. A simple, common model of a lagging actuator or filter is the first-order low-pass,

$$ H(f) = \frac{1}{1 + i f / f_c}, $$

with cutoff frequency $f_c$. Its magnitude is $|H(f)| = 1/\sqrt{1+(f/f_c)^2}$, which equals $1/\sqrt{2}$ (about $71\%$, the conventional bandwidth edge) exactly at $f=f_c$, and its phase $\arg H(f) = -\arctan(f/f_c)$ lags further and further behind as $f$ grows. The Control tab's feedback-lag toggle applies exactly this $H(f)$ to every Fourier coefficient.

3. The link: an arm is a Fourier series

Compare §1.1's synthesis formula to §2.1's forward kinematics with constant spin rates — they're the same equation. Reading off the correspondence term by term:

Fourier component $k$↔Link $k$ of the arm
amplitude $|c_k|$↔link length $r_k$
frequency $f_k$↔constant spin rate of joint $k$
phase $\varphi_k$↔link's angle at $t=0$
partial sum over $k$↔the chain of links, tip to tail
full sum $z(t)$↔end-effector position $p_n(t)$

One shorthand worth being careful about: it's tempting to say "the radius is the frequency," but that's not quite right — a link's length is its amplitude $|c_k|$, a completely different quantity from its spin rate $f_k$. It's an easy slip to make (both numbers are "how big is this component," in different senses), which is exactly why the demo always labels them separately.

3.1 Relative motor speed, revisited

§2.4's relative angular velocity $\dot q_k = 2\pi(f_k - f_{k-1})$ now has a clean Fourier reading: it's the gap between consecutive links' frequencies. Sort the links by frequency and adjacent motors move slowly; keep them in an arbitrary order and neighboring motors can be asked to spin arbitrarily fast even though every link's motion, viewed from the fixed world frame, is a perfectly gentle constant-rate rotation.

3.2 Truncation is a bandwidth limit

Keeping only $|f| \le B$ frequencies (the Control tab's bandwidth slider, or Draw mode's top-$K$ selection restricted to low frequencies) is exactly a low-pass filter on the path. Physically, it's what you'd get from an arm whose actuators simply cannot spin fast enough to realize any $f_k$ larger than $B$ — a limited-bandwidth actuator turns into a limited-frequency Fourier reconstruction, corners and all (see §1.5's Gibbs ripple).

3.3 Dragging a joint, and why the rest of the chain follows

When you drag joint $k$'s tip to a new point $P$ at the current time $t$, the demo only changes link $k$'s own amplitude and phase, by inverting the forward-kinematics formula for that one link:

$$ w = \big(P - p_{k-1}(t)\big)\, e^{-i2\pi f_k t}, \qquad |c_k| = |w|, \qquad \varphi_k = \arg(w). $$

($p_{k-1}(t)$, the base of link $k$, is unaffected by this — it only depends on links $1,\dots,k-1$.) Every link downstream of $k$ (links $k+1,\dots,n$) is defined relative to $p_k(t)$, so once $p_k(t)$ jumps to $P$, they are rigidly carried along with it, exactly as if you'd grabbed the middle of a real jointed arm and the far end came with your hand. Nothing about their own amplitudes, frequencies, or phases changes — only their position in the world does.

3.4 Feedback lag as a Fourier filter

Turning on feedback lag applies §2.6's $H(f)$ to every coefficient at once: $c_f \mapsto H(f)\, c_f$. Since $|H(f)|<1$ and $\arg H(f)<0$ for $f>0$ (and mirrored for $f<0$), each component shrinks and rotates — more so the higher its frequency — so the whole traced shape shrinks inward and lags behind where it "should" be, exactly the signature of a real feedback loop chasing a fast-moving target through a slow actuator.

3.5 Where the analogy breaks down

It's a genuinely useful analogy, not a literal equivalence. Real arms have hard joint limits (a motor can't spin forever, only through some finite range), real dynamics (inertia, torque limits, and gravity couple every joint's required torque to the state and acceleration of every other joint, nonlinearly — unlike our independent, decoupled phasors), and real trajectories are almost never literally sums of constant-rate rotations; they're planned to meet task goals (reach here, avoid that) and are usually far from periodic. Treat the Fourier-arm picture as a clean toy model that makes the frequency-domain intuitions (bandwidth, low-pass filtering, redundancy) tangible, not as a description of how industrial robots are actually driven.
