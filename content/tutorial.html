This page is a playground for one idea: a robot arm whose joints spin at constant rates is a Fourier series, drawn by hand. The tutorial below walks through the Fourier-analysis side, the control-theory side, and then the dictionary that translates between them. Skim the "quick tour" first, then come back to any section while you play with the demo above.

0. Quick tour

The demo has three tabs.

  • Arm. An animated chain of links (the "puppet") and its power/amplitude spectrum. They are two views of the same data. The default preset draws the chain as a human arm: each Fourier component is a bone (upper arm, forearm, hand, finger, fingertip), and a skin outline bends around the shoulder, elbow, wrist, and knuckle joints. In this human-arm view, a plain drag rotates the bone: its length stays fixed, and the joint swings to follow your pointer's direction. Shift-drag also changes its length, landing the joint under the pointer. Untick Show arm skin to work with the bare links, where a plain drag does the full move (length and angle together).
  • Draw. Sketch any closed-ish shape freehand; the app decomposes it into rotating components and re-draws it as spinning links, the way 3Blue1Brown's epicycle animations do.
  • Control. The filtered arm, relabeled with control-theory language: joint-angle traces (absolute or relative/motor angle, wrapped or unwrapped), a bandwidth (low-pass) slider, and an optional feedback-lag filter, both of which are applied before the arm and traces are drawn. This filtered arm is a read-only display; drag joints in the Arm tab instead.

Interactions that work wherever the interactive arm or spectrum view appears:

  • Drag a joint's tip in the Arm tab to move it; every link further out on the chain stays rigidly attached and slides along with it (see §3.3 for how the human-arm view's plain drag differs, rotating rather than relocating).
  • Drag a stem in the spectrum view: vertical motion changes that component's amplitude, horizontal motion changes its frequency (it snaps to the nearest integer). To change phase instead, hold Alt/Option while dragging, drag the small ring around a selected stem's head, or scroll the wheel over it.
  • Double-click empty space in the spectrum to add a new component; shift-drop one stem onto another at the same frequency to merge them into one (their coefficients add); select a stem and press Delete or Backspace to remove it.
  • In Draw mode, just draw on the canvas. The K slider controls how many of the strongest components, by amplitude, are kept in the reconstruction, so you can watch the sketch resolve as K grows.
  • In Control, the bandwidth slider and the feedback lag toggle both apply at once: bandwidth truncates the series to frequencies within it, and lag applies a first-order low-pass filter to every remaining component. The spectrum panel plots $|c_f|$ with the passband shaded and always overlays $|H(f)|$, whether or not the lag toggle is on.

1. Fourier analysis

1.1 Rotating phasors and the Fourier series

Treat the plane as the complex plane, so a path is a complex-valued function of time $z(t)$. A phasor at frequency $f$ is a unit-magnitude rotation $e^{i2\pi f t}$: as $t$ increases from $0$ to $1$, it sweeps once around the unit circle counter-clockwise if $f>0$. Scaling it by a complex coefficient $c_f = |c_f|e^{i\varphi_f}$ stretches it to radius $|c_f|$ and starts it at angle $\varphi_f$ when $t=0$.

A (complex) Fourier series is a sum of such phasors, one per integer frequency:

$$ z(t) = \sum_{f=-\infty}^{\infty} c_f\, e^{i2\pi f t}, \qquad t \in [0,1). $$

Every $c_f$ traces its own little circle at its own constant rate; the point you actually see, $z(t)$, is the running sum: the tip of the last phasor, chained onto the tip of the one before it. That chaining is how the arm view is drawn.

1.2 Recovering the coefficients: orthogonality

The phasors are orthonormal over one period,

$$ \int_0^1 e^{i2\pi f t}\, e^{-i2\pi g t}\, dt = \begin{cases}1 & f = g\\ 0 & f \ne g\end{cases} \quad (f, g \in \mathbb{Z}), $$

so multiplying $z(t)$ by $e^{-i2\pi f t}$ and integrating kills every term except the one we want:

$$ \int_0^1 z(t)\, e^{-i2\pi f t}\, dt = \sum_g c_g \int_0^1 e^{i2\pi (g-f) t}\, dt = c_f. $$

That integral is the analysis formula; the sum in §1.1 is the synthesis formula. They are inverses of each other.

1.3 From an integral to the DFT

We only ever have finitely many samples, so the demo replaces the integral with a sum over $N$ uniformly-spaced samples $z_n = z(n/N)$, $n = 0,\dots,N-1$:

$$ c_f = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1} z_n\, e^{-i2\pi f n / N}, \qquad f = -\tfrac{N}{2}, \dots, \tfrac{N}{2}-1, $$

which is the discrete Fourier transform (DFT), normalized so that $c_f$ has the same units and meaning as the continuous coefficient above.

Why resample by arc length? When you draw freehand, the pointer reports points unevenly in time: slow strokes pile up samples, fast strokes skip over whole stretches of curve. The DFT above assumes evenly-spaced samples of a single lap around the curve; feeding it raw, unevenly-timed pointer samples would silently over-weight the parts of the shape you drew slowly. So before taking the DFT, the drawn curve is closed up and resampled at $N$ points equally spaced by cumulative arc length, which depends only on the shape you drew, not on how fast your hand moved.

1.4 Amplitude vs. power, and Parseval

$|c_f|$ is the amplitude spectrum: it's the radius of link $f$ in the arm view. $|c_f|^2$ is the power spectrum. They rank components identically (power is a monotonic function of amplitude), but power adds up meaningfully: Parseval's theorem says

$$ \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1} |z_n|^2 \;=\; \sum_{f} |c_f|^2, $$

so the mean squared "size" of the path is partitioned across frequencies. The power spectrum is a budget, and truncating to the top-$K$ components (as Draw mode does, ranking by amplitude rather than frequency) keeps as much of that budget as possible for a given number of links.

1.5 Convergence, corners, and Gibbs' phenomenon

As you keep more terms (raise $K$, or widen the bandwidth in the Control tab), the partial sum converges to $z(t)$. Every path this demo draws is a closed, continuous curve, and for those the convergence is uniform: the whole curve gets close everywhere, not just at each point separately. A corner, like a square's corner or a heart's cusp, is a kink in the path, not a break in it: $z(t)$ itself never jumps there. Its Fourier coefficients decay more slowly, like $1/f^2$, so a corner takes more terms to sharpen than a smooth stretch does, but there is no persistent overshoot: as $K$ grows, corners round off and then sharpen, and the curve gets closer to the target shape everywhere.

Gibbs' phenomenon is a different, stronger effect that needs an actual jump discontinuity in the signal, not merely a corner in a path. The textbook case is a 1-D square wave as a function of time: at the instant the value jumps from $-1$ to $+1$, every partial sum overshoots by about $9\%$ of the jump, no matter how many terms you add; the ringing gets narrower but never shorter. None of the closed paths in this demo have that kind of jump (even closing the curve back on itself is a continuous move), so you won't see that persistent ring here; what you'll see instead is corners getting crisper as $K$ grows.

Try this. In Draw, sketch a shape with one sharp corner (a triangle is enough) and set $K$ to its lowest value. Raise $K$ slowly and watch small ripples appear near the corner and shrink toward zero as $K$ grows, consistent with the "kink, not a jump" distinction above — unlike Gibbs' phenomenon, where the overshoot stays pinned near $9\%$ no matter how many terms you add. Then load the square preset and compare: the whole shape is built from corners, and every one behaves the same way.

1.6 Negative frequencies spin the other way

$f>0$ makes $e^{i2\pi f t}$ sweep counter-clockwise as $t$ grows. Flip the sign, $f<0$, and $e^{i2\pi f t}=e^{-i2\pi |f| t}$ sweeps clockwise instead. A real-valued signal (say, just the $x$-coordinate of a drawing) needs $c_{-f} = \overline{c_f}$: the coefficient at $-f$ is the complex conjugate of the one at $f$, same amplitude and opposite phase, so their imaginary parts cancel. A complex, two-sided path (like an arm's end effector) has no such constraint and is free to use $+f$ and $-f$ asymmetrically; that asymmetry is what makes the traced path curl one way more than the other.

2. Control theory

2.1 A planar serial manipulator

Picture a chain of $n$ rigid links connected by revolute (rotating) joints, all lying flat in the plane, base pinned at the origin. Link $k$ has a fixed length $r_k$ and, at time $t$, points in the absolute direction $\theta_k(t)$ (measured from a fixed reference axis, same for every link). Adding the link vectors tip-to-tail gives forward kinematics: the position of every joint, including the end effector, as a sum of complex link vectors:

$$ p_k(t) = \sum_{j=1}^{k} r_j\, e^{i\theta_j(t)}. $$

If each joint spins at a constant absolute rate, $\theta_k(t) = \varphi_k + 2\pi f_k t$, this is identical in form to the Fourier partial sum in §1.1, with $c_k = r_k e^{i\varphi_k}$. That's the whole analogy in one equation; §3 spells out the dictionary.

2.2 Joint space vs. task space

The $n$ joint angles together are joint space: an $n$-dimensional description of the arm's configuration. What we usually care about (where the end effector actually is, $p_n(t)$) lives in 2-dimensional task space. Forward kinematics is the map from joint space to task space; it is easy (just evaluate the sum above) and always well defined.

2.3 Inverse kinematics and redundancy

Going the other way (given a target point $P$ in task space, find joint angles that reach it) is inverse kinematics, and it is generally hard. When $n>2$, joint space (dimension $n$) is bigger than task space (dimension $2$): infinitely many joint configurations put the tip at the same $P$. Such an arm is redundant, and the leftover $n-2$ degrees of freedom (formally, the null space of the arm's Jacobian) can move the joints around without moving the end effector at all.

Dragging in the arm view solves the smallest possible inverse-kinematics problem: moving joint $k$'s own tip to a point $P$ changes only link $k$'s amplitude and phase (two unknowns for two coordinates), leaving every other link's own parameters untouched; see §3.3 for the formula and why the rest of the chain still moves rigidly.

2.4 Absolute vs. relative (motor) angles

$\theta_k$ above is an absolute angle, measured against the fixed world frame. But a real motor at joint $k$ is bolted to link $k-1$, not to the world: it can only sense and drive the angle between link $k$ and link $k-1$. That's the relative (motor) angle,

$$ q_k(t) = \theta_k(t) - \theta_{k-1}(t), \qquad \theta_0 \equiv 0. $$

With constant absolute rates $\theta_k = \varphi_k + 2\pi f_k t$, the motor's angular velocity is $\dot q_k = 2\pi (f_k - f_{k-1})$, the difference of neighboring frequencies, not $f_k$ itself. Two adjacent links can each be spinning fast in the world frame while the motor between them barely moves, because $f_k \approx f_{k-1}$.

2.5 Feedback, frequency response, and Bode plots

A feedback controller measures the error between a desired output and the actual one and corrects the actuator accordingly; how well it tracks a signal typically depends on that signal's frequency content. The standard way to describe this is the system's frequency response $H(f)$: feed in $e^{i2\pi f t}$, and (for a linear, time-invariant system) the steady-state output is $H(f)\, e^{i2\pi f t}$: same frequency, but scaled by $|H(f)|$ and shifted in phase by $\arg H(f)$. A Bode plot shows $|H(f)|$ and $\arg H(f)$ against $f$, and is the standard way engineers read off how a controller will treat any given frequency at a glance.

2.6 Bandwidth and the first-order lag

A system's bandwidth is (roughly) the range of frequencies it can track without much attenuation or delay; components above it get squashed. A simple, common model of a lagging actuator or filter is the first-order low-pass,

$$ H(f) = \frac{1}{1 + i f / f_c}, $$

with cutoff frequency $f_c$. Its magnitude is $|H(f)| = 1/\sqrt{1+(f/f_c)^2}$, which equals $1/\sqrt{2}$ (about $71\%$, the conventional bandwidth edge) at $f=f_c$, and its phase $\arg H(f) = -\arctan(f/f_c)$ lags further and further behind as $f$ grows. The Control tab's feedback-lag toggle applies this $H(f)$ to every Fourier coefficient.

Try this. On the Control tab, turn on the feedback-lag toggle and lower the cutoff $f_c$ until it sits just below your fastest joint's frequency. The arm and $\theta$ panel both now show the filtered arm: $c_k \mapsto H(f_k)\, c_k$ shrinks that link's length by $|H(f_k)|$ and adds a constant phase offset $\arg H(f_k) = -\arctan(f_k/f_c)$ to its starting angle. Watch the link visibly shorten in the arm view, and on the $\theta$ trace watch its line shift downward by that same constant offset — the line's slope (the joint's spin rate $f_k$) is untouched, since lag rotates and scales each coefficient without changing its frequency.

3. The link: an arm is a Fourier series

Compare §1.1's synthesis formula to §2.1's forward kinematics with constant spin rates: they're the same equation. Reading off the correspondence term by term, using $c_k$ for the coefficient of link $k$ (spinning at frequency $f_k$), gives the dictionary below, which the rest of this section spells out in detail:

Fourier component $k$↔Link $k$ of the arm
amplitude $|c_k|$↔link length $r_k$
frequency $f_k$↔constant spin rate of joint $k$
phase $\varphi_k$↔link's angle at $t=0$
partial sum over $k$↔the chain of links, tip to tail
full sum $z(t)$↔end-effector position $p_n(t)$

One shorthand worth being careful about: it's tempting to say "the radius is the frequency," but that's not quite right. A link's length is its amplitude $|c_k|$, a different quantity from its spin rate $f_k$. It's an easy slip to make (both numbers are "how big is this component," in different senses), which is why the demo always labels them separately.

3.1 Relative motor speed, revisited

§2.4's relative angular velocity $\dot q_k = 2\pi(f_k - f_{k-1})$ now has a clean Fourier reading: it's the gap between consecutive links' (signed) frequencies. The editor's Sort by f button orders links by signed frequency, which minimizes every adjacent $\Delta f$ and keeps neighboring motors moving as slowly as possible; sorting by $|f|$ or leaving them in an arbitrary order can instead put a large-negative and large-positive frequency side by side, asking neighboring motors to spin arbitrarily fast even though every link's own motion, viewed from the fixed world frame, is a perfectly gentle constant-rate rotation.

3.2 Truncation is a bandwidth limit

Keeping only $|f| \le B$ frequencies, the Control tab's bandwidth slider, is a low-pass filter on the path: every low-frequency component survives and every high-frequency one is dropped, regardless of size. Physically, it's what you'd get from an arm whose actuators cannot spin fast enough to realize any $f_k$ larger than $B$; a limited-bandwidth actuator turns into a limited-frequency Fourier reconstruction, corners and all (see §1.5 for what more terms do to those corners as $B$ grows).

Draw mode's $K$ slider is a different kind of truncation. Top-$K$ keeps the $K$ largest-amplitude components, whatever their frequency, so it will keep a high-frequency component that carries real energy while dropping a low-frequency one that barely contributes. That makes top-$K$ the more efficient way to approximate a shape with a fixed number of links; bandwidth limiting is the more physically realistic one, since a real actuator's speed limit has no notion of how much a component contributes to the shape.

3.3 Dragging a joint, and why the rest of the chain follows

All of this happens on the Arm tab; the Control tab's arm is a read-only display. With bare links shown (skin off, or shift held in the human-arm view), dragging joint $k$'s tip to a new point $P$ at the current time $t$ changes both link $k$'s amplitude and phase, by inverting the forward-kinematics formula for that one link:

$$ w = \big(P - p_{k-1}(t)\big)\, e^{-i2\pi f_k t}, \qquad |c_k| = |w|, \qquad \varphi_k = \arg(w). $$

That lands the joint on $P$. In the human-arm (skin) view, though, a bone can't change length, so a plain drag rotates it only: $|c_k|$ stays fixed and just the phase updates, so the joint follows the pointer's direction rather than its exact position:

$$ \varphi_k(t) = \arg\!\Big[\big(P - p_{k-1}(t)\big)\, e^{-i2\pi f_k t}\Big]. $$

Shift-drag in the skin view falls back to the full inversion above, changing length too and landing the joint on $P$.

In both cases, $p_{k-1}(t)$, the base of link $k$, is unaffected: it only depends on links $1,\dots,k-1$. Every link downstream of $k$ (links $k+1,\dots,n$) is defined relative to $p_k(t)$, so once $p_k(t)$ moves, they are rigidly carried along with it, as if you'd grabbed the middle of a real jointed arm and the far end came with your hand. Nothing about their own amplitudes, frequencies, or phases changes; only their position in the world does.

3.4 Feedback lag as a Fourier filter

Turning on feedback lag applies §2.6's $H(f)$ to every coefficient at once: $c_k \mapsto H(f_k)\, c_k$. Since $|H(f)|<1$ and $\arg H(f)<0$ for $f>0$ (and mirrored for $f<0$), each component shrinks and rotates, more so the higher its frequency, so the whole traced shape shrinks inward and lags behind where it "should" be: the signature of a real feedback loop chasing a fast-moving target through a slow actuator.

3.5 The human-arm view, and where the analogy breaks down

The default Arm-tab preset draws the chain as a human arm: each component is a bone, upper arm, forearm, hand, finger, and fingertip, and the skin is a single outline that deforms around the shoulder, elbow, wrist, and knuckle joints as the bones move (see §3.3 for how dragging a bone in this view differs from dragging a bare link). It's a useful picture precisely because it's an imperfect one: a real shoulder or elbow is a revolute joint with a hard range of motion, not a motor free to spin forever, so it can never sustain the constant-rate rotation this demo's phasors assume. Watch the skin as $t$ runs past a full period, or scrub $t$ manually: the "bones" keep spinning straight through ranges no real joint could reach, which is the point where the toy model and a real arm part ways.

More generally, this is a useful analogy, not a literal equivalence. Real arms have hard joint limits, real dynamics (inertia, torque limits, and gravity couple every joint's required torque to the state and acceleration of every other joint, nonlinearly, unlike our independent, decoupled phasors), and real trajectories are almost never actually sums of constant-rate rotations; they're planned to meet task goals (reach here, avoid that) and are usually far from periodic. Treat the Fourier-arm picture as a clean toy model that makes the frequency-domain intuitions (bandwidth, low-pass filtering, redundancy) tangible, not as a description of how industrial robots are actually driven.
